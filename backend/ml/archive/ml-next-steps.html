Your rubric — the 5 situations

Written. Keep this open while labelling; it also becomes an appendix in your paper.

Productive Both students are working on the same problem together. The driver is typing, the navigator is actively contributing out loud, and they hand the keyboard back and forth. Usually: steady editing, navigator sending real messages, roles swapping every few minutes, runs mostly passing. But: quiet doesn't mean unproductive — a pair thinking through something hard may go silent while still working together. And fast, successful runs with no discussion often means one person racing ahead alone: that's dominance.

Driver dominance One student has held the keyboard for a long stretch while the other stays verbally engaged. Because either can take the driver role at any time, this is a shared failure to rotate, not one person blocking the other. Usually: no role switch for a long stretch, while the navigator keeps sending real messages. But: early on nobody has swapped yet — that's normal. Judge against how long they've been working: no swap at minute 6 is nothing, at minute 25 it's the whole story.

Passive navigator The navigator isn't contributing at all. Not suggesting, not questioning, not thinking aloud. The driver works alone. Usually: no messages from the navigator while the driver works steadily. But: read what they wrote, not how much. Five messages of "ok", "yeah", "sure" is passive despite the count. One message of "wait — that loop starts at 1, should it?" is real contribution. Judge content, not number.

Logic struggle Both are working and talking, but stuck — repeated attempts, no progress. Usually: several failed runs in a row, discussion continuing, edits happening but the code not getting closer. But: failing a lot isn't the same as being stuck. Systematic debugging — change one thing, test, narrow down — is productive even with many failures. Struggle looks like repeating the same fix, or chat showing confusion rather than ideas.

Disengaged Neither student is really working on the problem any more. Long gaps, little typing, little talk. Usually: very few actions in the window, almost no discussion. But: quiet-and-working looks the same on counts as quiet-and-gone — if the code is steadily improving through the silence, that's focus. And separate it from struggle: a stuck pair is still trying; a disengaged pair has stopped.

Check them in this order

Disengaged -> Logic struggle -> Passive navigator -> Driver dominance -> otherwise Productive

A window can genuinely look like two things. The order settles it. Struggle beats dominance because a hint helps more than a role nudge; passive beats dominance because a silent navigator is the more specific problem. Without a fixed order, two people can follow the same rules and still disagree.

When you can't tell, skip it The labelling tool has a skip key. An honest skip costs one window; a forced wrong label teaches the model something false.

Phrases like "a long stretch" and "several" are deliberately loose right now — you can't pick real thresholds before seeing real sessions.

Label about 20 windows, check whether the wording matched what you actually saw, adjust, then continue. Write down that you did this — revising a rubric against early data is normal methodology and worth a sentence in your paper, not something to hide.
